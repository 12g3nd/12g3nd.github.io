A lot about succeeding in commerce isn't about attending lectures or acing finals, much of it has to do with building connections and playing the "game." What I mean by that is doing the charades. Standing in line to network with recruiters, posting humblebrags on LinkedIn in artificial intelligence prose, and shooting an unnatural cold email that attempts to subvert the expectations of some random executive but fails to do so miserably. If I'm sounding passive aggressive here, let me remove all doubt and just say it: I hate this down to my very core. It infuriates me more than it should, makes me feel like I'm falling behind when I don't conform, and makes me loathe myself when I do partake in it.

One word that I have a love-hate relationship with is *performative*. As a business student, I'm expected to fit a certain mold, but for the most part I don't. I care about the arts (I've been writing poetry for years now), and if you ask me what my favourite course was during first-year, I'd say Computational Biology Foundations (CSB195) over some generic business course. I don't care whether what someone likes is normal or not, passion and authenticity matter far more to me than fitting a shape. So I hate whenever someone calls me performative, or thinks that what I'm doing is performative. My individuality is one of the key pillars of who I am, and while I don't make what makes me different my entire personality, it's undeniably a part of it. This, however, is a double-edged sword. I know too many people who bolt on some distinct thing they don't really care about just to seem more interesting to others. It's a weird paradox, where I hate people I perceive as performative, and I also hate being called performative.

When I was in Grade 10, I was really excited to get LinkedIn because I thought it made me seem more mature, a big step towards being an "adult." For a while I just connected with suggested profiles and accepted whoever added me, and now I have well over a thousand connections, but if we're being real, I barely know a lot of them. I remember reading online about why others despised it and dismissing the criticism, because I figured it gets better, and that they just weren't using it well. These days, I genuinely can't wait for the day I never have to open LinkedIn ever again. It's just that when I open the app on my phone or the site on my laptop, I see a post that was completely written by ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini, and it feels so soulless. At least rewrite parts of it, or humanize it enough to show a bit of effort. There are cool people on there whose posts are genuinely interesting, an amazing personal achievement or some fascinating research, but they're buried under a mountain of nonsense. For example, here's the kind of thing I'd probably see:

<blockquote className="fake-post">
  <p>I was rejected today.</p>
  <p>Not by a client. Not by an investor.</p>
  <p>By a 19-year-old named Kayla at a drive-thru window.</p>
  <p>"We stopped serving breakfast at 10:30," she said.</p>

I think the system deserves much of the blame here as the culture students are forced to enter encourages this flattening of dimensionality. Everyone has unique interests, funny stories, things that make them special. I have it, you have it, everyone I've ever known has had it. But the powder keg forces people to churn out slop instead. There's an irony around the performative uniqueness that isn't lost on me, when people add "quirks" just to seem more interesting, when business culture has commodified "authenticity" to the point where even being unique becomes a performance metric.

I admit I'm not immune to any of this. I succumb to the pressure-cooker environment more often than I'd like, doom scrolling LinkedIn profiles to see what I'm up against. There's this person I know in my program — who I knew before it, too — and while there are brief moments where we act like friends, I always feel a need to keep my guard up. I can never be honest about my struggles, because it highlights my weaknesses, and I can't talk about my wins easily either, because it just feels like trying to one-up them. Recently I got a call from them and we talked for a bit, and on paper a summary of it would read as two friends catching up. The call itself felt more like "let's see where the people I deem competition are at." And that's the part that actually gets to me: the LinkedIn slop is a mask you wear for people who don't matter, this was a mask worn one-on-one with someone who's supposed to know me. The most performative thing in my life isn't a post written by some AI. It's a conversation orchestrated to look like friendship. The thing is, I'm very ambitious, and I like and respect other people when they're ambitious too. But that all goes out the window the second someone treats success as zero-sum, where if they win, I have to lose, and vice versa. Talking to people who only want to hear about my failures to validate themselves or my accomplishments so they can copy my strategies is entirely exhausting.

There's a line from John Adams' letter to his wife Abigail that I think about more than I'd like to admit. I'll [paraphrase](https://www.masshist.org/digitaladams/archive/doc?id=L17800512jasecond) but he essentially writes that he must study politics and war, so that his sons may have the liberty to study mathematics and philosophy, so that *their* children might have the right to study painting, poetry, and music. Three generations just to earn the right to make something potentially beautiful instead of something useful. It's why I tell myself the coffee chats and the hundred cold emails are the price I pay so my kids, and their kids, won't have to.

I don't even know if authenticity actually wins in the long run, or if that's just the story people who suck at the game tell themselves to sleep at night. I really want it to be true. My friends at other business programs tell me they manage a coffee chat a week, and I suppose that's impressive, but to me it sounds like saying "always down to connect!" to a stranger while meaning it the way you mean "we should get dinner sometime" — which is to say, not at all, and you both know it, and neither of you says so. Then again, I use a decent bit of nepotism to skip that line entirely, friends' parents, mostly, and I don't really know how to reconcile that with everything I've just said. I get to be honest and open with people who've known me for a while in a way I could never be with a stranger, and that's a safety net most people networking their way up don't have. I probably don't get to sit on this high horse with quite as much standing as I'd like.

<p className="lede">So, here's where I actually am.</p>

I hate the game, and I refuse to fully play it the way I'm expected to. Some days that refusal feels like conviction and other days it just feels like I'm last on a scoreboard I claim not to believe in. Knowing me, I'll probably open LinkedIn after publishing this transmission.
