I wake up and can see the CN Tower from my bedroom window. That's pretty cool. It's a lot less cool when it's Friday night and I feel that I should be out and about. At nineteen, in my second year of university, I manage to get in my head too much. About a lot of things, but it stings the most when it's a combination of jealousy, envy, and resentment. Perhaps that's perennially who I am. I'll give you an example. I came across an article from a different university's student paper where they describe how going to a university in a college town is the absolute best. I wanted to be mad, like I feel that they are so viscerally wrong, that going to school downtown in the biggest city in the country is just as valid of an experience if not more. I felt like they were rubbing it in my face. Moving to Toronto for university feels like skipping straight into your twenties (people networking, talking "shop," drifting between classes). A university town like London is what high schoolers dream of because it's basically an extended teenage playground. I know the grass is greener on the other side and other cute idioms, that my school is good, and that living in the city at nineteen has its own really cool benefits, but I still can't help feeling like *man...*

It's "Halloweekend" (weekend of Halloween), and I spent $70 on a Cyclops (X-Men) mask, and I don't even know what I'm doing, whether I'm even going out. I feel almost ashamed to admit that because college guys want to go out, want to go on a bender, and do crazy things. I don't do the college rites of passage like school spirit, homecoming, and whatever else. I just want to chill at home most days, building something new, or playing Rocket League on my PS5.

I live in a big city. I'll probably live in a big city for the next decade. I don't have a bar like The Ceeps or Delilah's (Dells if you have the swag for it) I can say I'm going to, and everyone else knows what I'm talking about.

I don't remember a lot of things clearly before COVID-19, like some sort of 'brain fog,' which does concern me. However, my constant worrying about the other ninety-nine problems in my life has rendered that moot. Mainly the ones I overthink and under-do. 2020 is a good checkpoint in time, however; I turned thirteen that year. I remember staying up till midnight, waiting (uncommon for me at the time, all too common now), watching an episode of Austin & Ally on Disney+, my mom next to me; life felt like it was about to get *real*. The advent of idealized adolescence. For the next few months, my life consisted of the following routine: playing Call of Duty: Modern Warfare, Fortnite, Minecraft, and a plethora of random games, checking Google Classroom after noon, and letting my hair grow far too long. I'm getting nostalgic typing this out.

I was ready, but I had a doubt in the back of my mind that I wouldn't get the "teenage experience."

In Grade 11, I started becoming close with a new group of friends, composed mainly of people I hadn't known before high school. Let's call this group "16 yutes," although the number (which represented the number of people in the Snapchat group chat) and the noun (in the example 'yutes') changed frequently. It felt great, hanging out at malls, meeting up after school, developing our own cant; the entire experience reminded me of elementary school. I was just a lot more socially awkward and aware, so it felt difficult. Seventeen-year-old me had to unlearn, relearn, and plain learn a lot of things.

In elementary school, I had a close group of friends called "WeafV," which stood for "We eat apples from Vietnam." Don't ask me why, I barely remember it, something of an inside joke between ten edgy pre-teens. WeafV was actually the group chat name on Discord; we existed as a group of friends for a number of years till that point. That was back when I didn't care. Girls didn't matter. Grades didn't determine your university. Maybe I did, a little, and my rose-tinted lens blurs my past, but I was optimistic.

A year after joining the "16 yutes" group, I started to get used to the whole ordeal. Grade 12 was a great year for me, for the most part. I expanded the people I knew and the experiences I went through insanely quick. I became more talkative, developed a distinct personality, and just felt more comfortable. One of the things I'm most proud of was the variety of people I knew; I was friends with all sorts of people who connected with different aspects of me. The trade-off was hard; my grades slipped a bit. I stopped caring as much as I used to. My drive, and much of my ambition in those aspects had vanished. I should probably consider that the "rebel" phase I never had, but always saw. A watered-down suburban legend of sorts. I talked to people I hadn't had proper conversations with in five years at that point, went to basements, and developed a nicotine addiction. Not via peer pressure per se, but the pressure I put on myself. I first vaped, smoked weed, and got drunk all within the same semester: the last four months of high school. I wouldn't categorize that as regret, though.

One thing I heard from the other people in my grade was regarding the experience of attending a party, which I usually heard about afterwards. A high school party like a backyard and living room, with booze, cup pong, a pool, and loud music. I was never cool enough to get invited, and my demons convinced me to skip open invites. The first party I attended was the one after graduation — the ceremony, which for some reason was a month before the actual last day of class. I remember turning to my friend in the car before we arrived and telling him, "I'm kind of scared, this is the first party I'm ever going to." He was surprised, but he told me to chill. We went in, greeted by girls I don't know if I talked to once, went into the living room, greeted by my boys. That was when I knew: this was going to be alright. We did a lot of dumb things that night like spontaneous push-up contests, downing a Corona, and taking some candid pictures with digital cameras. I had a good night, but inevitably felt a tinge of sadness that I missed out on this for the previous four-ish years. My trademark, taking a good experience, and finding a way to become upset about it. I went to a lot more parties that June, and at least got to check that off my list. Still, in that "crammed Spring," I was finally in the circle, but I still felt like an imposter watching through glass.

These days, I feel conflicted. I think of next May when I turn twenty and I feel scared. I'm older, I'll never be a teenager again. Were those really the best moments of my life? There's no way I peaked at eighteen, right? When a fellow Rotman Commerce student asked me about the most dangerous situation I've ever been in, I didn't know what to say. I wanted to impress him, even slightly, so he didn't think less of me, as if doing something dangerous somehow made me more of a man. I don't have a lot of cool lore and unselfconscious recklessness that I could really tell around a campfire or anything, nothing that could fit inside a movie trailer. The most dangerous thing I've probably done is jumping into an abandoned quarry last June. And that's more of a mild violation than something awesome. I fluffed the story up anyway. That's what shame looks like. And losing a piece of integrity.

I was born here (Columbus, but close enough), but I've grown up in a weird limbo of Indian-American-Canadian culture. I absorbed a lot of things, and it's widened my perspective, sure, but it's also dazed me a lot. Not unlike a lot of second-generation immigrants I suppose. You grow up consuming media and culture; you expect life to go one way, to assimilate fully, to forget your differences, struggle with identity, and become confused, all while going through the process of growing up. My family's world and the world I grew up in felt like they had nothing in common. Primarily, their version of what "being a teenager" meant differed from what was on my screen. I like to consider it as the adjacent topics of what my *origins* (my parents' house, the academic grind, a good son) and my *destination* (lakeshore wealth, charisma, effortless socializing) are. I still have a hard time classifying what fits in each category and, from what I can see, the best explanation is that it's a scale — no activity can fit in *origin* or *destination* cleanly. Although, I might be uncertain of that explanation too soon enough.

When I entered high school, I was locked-in on academics. I was a machine. Insane ceiling. In 'Greatest of All Time' conversations. Potential was sky-high. I was also alone, more than I have ever been. I had spent my Grade 8 online, in virtual school, and hadn't realized the toll it took on me. My lack of social skills, struggling with eye contact, acne, crooked posture, it was bad. There were probably a lot of people who felt similarly, but I felt like I didn't catch up at the same rate as everyone else. I met a lot of different groups of people, but hadn't connected strongly with any just yet.

Something I mourn of my old self is how much I didn't care about what others thought about me, for better and rarely for worse (the way I dressed, let's not discuss that further). Admittedly, a lot of my personality was derived from trying to be a contrarian and being anti-social. I didn't get Snapchat till the start of Grade 10, Instagram in Grade 11, and TikTok in Grade 12. I imagine the road not taken: me never using these apps, never giving in to the desire to fit in, never abandoning my fourteen-year-old self's principles.

Grade 10 was when I realized something had to change. My first semester was composed solely of Grade 11 courses, so if I thought Grade 9 was lonely, I had no idea. I had a different lunch schedule than my peers, and actually knew no-one. I made a few friends, achieved a 95%+ semester, but it was a really bad time. My second semester loosened a bit. I actually played Varsity Cricket, off the bench, but I was briefly and technically a Varsity athlete.

The balance between a social life and strong success in school has been the biggest challenge in my life. I say that mainly because I still haven't come close to solving it. That's why my jealousy flares when I see someone my age (a friend, a peer, someone else entirely) who accomplishes what I wanted and does so seamlessly. It breaks every notion I've internalized with the broken bargain of the "grind." The way I understood my parents' message in 2021 was pretty clear. No girlfriends, no drinking, just grind. Usually, kids who follow that script comfort themselves with serene lies. Something along the lines of: "I traded my teenage years for success, while the popular kids peaked in high school." Not that they'd ever say it out loud, at least the non-obnoxious ones, but they'd think it all the time. *The ugly thought*. Except you look at those "silver-spoon" (in quotes because I, too, am quite privileged in a lot of ways) friends and realize they got the girls (or whoever), the charisma, the fun "lore," the "perfect flaws," and their parents can still hand them top business connections on a gleaming platter. I saw it firsthand with internships last semester. While I was out applying to five a day, they were chilling, knowing they had something. It felt like they didn't have to pay the tax I paid.

Funny enough, I actually didn't plan on going to Rotman Commerce originally, and, honestly, I wasn't thrilled about going to the University of Toronto, but that's more so due to the aspect of staying in Canada for a prospective four more years (I wanted to go to the United States). I basically skipped my O-week ("Orientation week" or frosh), was commuting for the first few weeks, watched all my friends meet new people, while I wallowed in misery. I eventually decided to get a dorm, and even though I'm a University College student, I was given a room at New College (shout-out Wilson Hall, no shout-outs for the Dining Commons staff). I was paired with a stranger, a foreign student who didn't know how to speak or understand English. That was a mixed bag for sure, but at least it was memorable. I didn't know the people on my floor because I came late, and I dreaded Dining Hall because of my anxiety. Eventually I met a few people on my floor and played Avalon and poker, and invited a few people over to my room here and there, but I never had the "dorm" experience if that makes sense.

Luckily, I knew a few people, mainly from high school and all from Oakville, so it wasn't like I had no-one to talk to.

I like to subscribe to the idea that we need to abandon the concept of "typical teenage experiences" and the deadline of having them before the ripe age of twenty. You might call it cope, and I'd agree a little. That a lot of whether you get to have these experiences is via luck and chance. Regardless, I had that view challenged when some of these guys on my dorm floor invited me to get stoned at a fraternity or at Ripley's, and I excused myself, using finals as my reasoning. I couldn't let myself get too close because I was too scared; I felt like they didn't really like me, that I was out of place. I live a privileged life, even with all the aspects that can marginalize me. I remember feeling a pit in my stomach when I heard them laughing in the hallway from my room.

My time in Grade 10 reminds me of my current rut. I use the "locked-in" shield — my guard for when I start acting *"too locked in and focused on my own thing"* — and avoid "talking shop" (avoiding career conversations that can lead to ordinary social conversations). That might have accidentally caged me in a hyper-ambitious persona. Let's illustrate this: Person A introduces themselves to Person B and mentions a plan to go out to an event. Person B thinks Person A is just stating something, cannot read between the lines, and will never initiate a plan themselves. Person B uses the guise of wanting to work, even if they're not planning to, to prevent any further conversation. If Person A asks Person B about their work, Person B likely responds with "Enough about school, I don't even want to talk about it," laughing it off. I'm Person B a lot. The conversation is pleasant enough, but it will never lead to anything. I liken it to the same layer of protection that a sixteen-year-old Srihith used as he was stuck in a state of maladaptive daydreaming scenarios that consist of more balanced versions of himself.

Earlier this month, during Thanksgiving weekend, my parents and I took a weekend trip to London (yes, the other one, all the way in the land of "blokes"). I had an evening to myself, so I took the Elizabeth line from Maidenhead to Liverpool Street to watch a show at the Old Blue Last. I saw it was the type of vibe I wanted. I went way too early, walked around the Liverpool Street area for forty-five minutes, went back, drank some imported lager, and watched the show. I expected more. Young and alone in London, a place where no one knew me, I could be anyone, right? Not me, it seems. I thought I could at least go back with a memorable story or a conversation, or at least be a little less stiff. Instead, I went back earlier than expected. My phone died on the train, but I didn't really mind. I did enjoy the music, even if I was a bit of a social recluse. Honestly, whenever I see these experiences, I enjoy the parts I least expect and they always disappoint in the ways people say they're worth doing.

I think the aspect of teenagehood that bothers me most is this self-conceived notion of the expiring "grace period" of clumsy romance. I feel like there's an unspoken timeline where everyone figured it out before university started. I want the physical check boxes (first kiss, losing virginity) at my own pace, but sometimes I feel pressured to get them out of the way so I'm not "inexperienced" in my twenties. I'm afraid that going at my own pace will leave me feeling behind even in my twenties, doing things I feel I should've tried in my teens. Now, let's call this the Genie test, which asks what I would wish for even if no one else ever knew or found out. The "want it" rather than "wanted to have it" test. I want a beautiful, real first date where we talk all night. I've had crushes, sure, girls I found attractive, faux-dates, but never that. And that I'd give anything for, and one day, I hope to have it.

I sincerely hope getting older means I carry less shame about the things that haven't happened yet. I still want some of them, I know that for sure, but I'd like to get better at telling what I actually want from what I just feel I should've done by now.

I should probably emphasize something before I reinforce the wrong idea: I am happy with who I am, and I would never want to be anyone else. Not because I think I am better than everyone else, but I enjoy who and what I am. I may not be everything I want just yet, but I'll always be optimistic in that I can reach that ideal.

Nothing's going to change in six months, or maybe everything does. Maybe it'll free me. I mean I just wrote this entire transmission and I'm not sure I've let go of anything. To a twenty-five-year-old me, we have changed, will continue to change, but the tenets of our being remain the same. I'm sure that even after everything, regrets included, twelve-year-old me would still be proud of who I am right now, just as I will be of you. Anyway, I got to go brush my teeth and get going with my day, thanks for reading this.
