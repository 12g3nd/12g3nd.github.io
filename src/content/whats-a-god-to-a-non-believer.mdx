My mom still asks me to pray sometimes.

Usually it's before something important. Something I want to go well, or something she wants to go well for me. Like when I got laser eye surgery last Friday, which I guess we both wanted to go well. She'll tell me to put my hands together in front of Ganesha, specifically a statue of the god that sits neatly near the staircase, and I do. I always do. I stand there with my palms pressed against each other, slightly bent over, and look in the direction I'm supposed to look, my eyes never really registering what I am pretending to do. I don't think Ganesha can hear me so it doesn't really matter to me.

That should make the gesture easy to explain. It doesn't. I'm not praying in the way my mother is. I'm also not mocking her, and I don't feel like I'm lying to her either. If anything, the part I'm certain about is why I'm doing it. She believes something good may come from the prayer. I know something good comes from letting my mother feel that I'm safe, or protected, or whatever she thinks the prayer is doing for me. Sometimes pretending is the kindest thing you have to offer. The love was mine even if the prayer was anything but.

I have called myself non-religious since I was fourteen. "Non-believer" works too, at least for the purposes of the title. The problem with the word is that it makes belief sound cleaner than it has ever felt to me, as if there were a room marked RELIGION and at some point I simply walked out. I'm pretty sure I spent my entire life lingering in the doorway.

My first serious problem with religion was Jack Dawson. *Yes, seriously*.

I watched *Titanic* with my family when I was in Senior Kindergarten, and when Jack died (sorry for the spoilers) I discovered, with great inconvenience, that people die. I had obviously known the word before then. I understood that [dead animals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bambi) were dead and that [old people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Up_(2009_film)) eventually died and that cartoons sometimes had [parents who were dead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Futurama) before the movie even started. None of that had really forced me to deal with the possibility that one day I would stop existing too. Jack freezing to death in the Atlantic managed it.

I couldn't sleep properly for about a week. Or three. I remember getting stuck on the idea of nothingness. Not darkness, because darkness would still be something I could experience. Not floating around in a black void, because at least there would still be a me floating around to complain about it, sentient (and annoying). The thing that scared me was the state before I was born. Nothing to observe. Nobody home. No memories. It remains one of the few distinct memories I have from that age.

My parents tried to help in the way their parents probably would have helped them. Maybe death wasn't the end. Maybe there was another life.

Reincarnation was somehow worse.

If I came back and didn't remember anything, what exactly had been saved? I wanted my parents. I wanted to know who they were and find them again and hug them, and know that they still loved me. Telling me that some future person might contain some portion of whatever made me *me*, while knowing none of the things I knew and loving none of the people I loved, felt like replacing me and insisting I should be grateful. I was five.

These days I prefer the starfish.

At some point I heard that a starfish could live forever, or regenerate forever, or something along those lines. I have made a conscious decision not to look this up. It is entirely possible that I have misunderstood starfish biology for years. For someone who usually wants to know the answer to everything, I'm happy leaving this one alone. I really hope that there isn't some twisted scenario where never looking into this costs me a million dollars on a game show or something. *Please*.

My imaginary version of the starfish gets to stay alive for as long as it wants and then, presumably, stop when it has had enough. That's the kind of afterlife I would take.

I don't remember exactly when I first or last tried to talk to God, but I remember one attempt better than most.

In the summer of 2017, I was ten and visiting India with my family. We were staying at my paternal grandparents' new apartment in Vijayawada, and some kind of event was happening the next day. Religious, cultural, probably both. I was supposed to be the important person in it. I don't know what the ceremony was called. I probably should.

What I remember is the night before. I was sleeping on a thin blanket on a cold marble floor near my *pinni* and a lot of other relatives, and before I went to sleep I decided to pray. Likely the consequence of being bored — the ultimate crime — without a charged LG tablet. I don't remember the exact words. It was probably something eerily close to: *God, if you can hear me…* I felt stupid.

Then I decided maybe everybody felt stupid when they prayed and the religious people were simply better at pushing through it. My aunt noticed me. She laughed a little, not cruelly, and praised me for being devoted. And I liked that. That part is probably more important than whatever I said to God.

For once, I had done the religious thing on my own correctly and somebody I cared about was impressed and even in awe. I felt proud for a moment, followed by a much harder feeling to describe. I was being praised for something happening inside me that wasn't really happening inside me if that makes sense.

Later on that same trip, we stayed with family in Bangalore. My great-uncle had a reputation for being one of the most devout Hindus in the family, which meant, naturally, that ten-year-old me decided I was going to impress him too. Yes, I was into external validation during that trip a lot.

I asked him for a copy of the Bhagavad Gita.

That detail matters to me now. Nobody shoved it into my hands. Nobody forced me to read it as punishment or dragged me into some theological education I wanted no part of. I asked for it. I wanted to see if there was something everyone else had figured out that I hadn't.

He gave me an adult edition and an illustrated book with stories from Hindu tradition. Once I got home, my mom read some of the stories with me. I liked stories. I still do. The problem was that these ones felt like fairy tales we happened to believe.

That was when Greek mythology started bothering me. By that point, I had read a lot of Rick Riordan. I had no problem accepting that Zeus belonged to mythology. Poseidon belonged to mythology. Even Hades belonged to mythology. Gods with extraordinary powers doing impossible things belonged to mythology. Then I would look at religious stories and wonder what supposedly sacred truth had moved them into a different category.

I didn't conclude that religion was fake at ten years old. I wasn't exactly Bertrand Russell with a *Star Wars: The Clone Wars* lunch bag. I just couldn't find the border.

For a time, Christianity seemed easier.

I grew up in Oakville, mostly around kids who did things I associated with being Canadian in the most elementary-school sense possible. They played ice hockey. They celebrated Christmas. Some went to church. A lot of the media I consumed, primarily YouTube, treated Christianity as the default religion, which meant I briefly thought being Christian might make me fit into the picture better.

I didn't tell anyone. It wasn't some secret conversion arc. I wasn't living some *Life of Pi* scenario myself. I don't remember seriously believing in Jesus either. I think I wanted the normalcy more than the theology. At one point I thought you had to be Christian to celebrate Christmas.

Diwali felt different. I liked Diwali partly because it was one of the rare moments when something from my family's world appeared outside the house and was not in the background. Other people, especially my friends, knew what it was. There were lights and celebrations and some acknowledgment that people like us existed too.

I didn't have the language for separating culture from religion yet. I only knew that sometimes I wanted distance from Hinduism, and sometimes I wanted to defend it. By fourteen, I had already decided privately that I wasn't Hindu in any meaningful religious sense. Saying it was another matter entirely.

There was a pizza place in the No Frills plaza at Abbeywood and Pilgrims Way where my friends and I would occasionally go during lunch. I think the place is gone now. Maybe it changed names. I don't know, I don't live there anymore. At some point, sitting there with about five friends, [religion came up](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/im-14-and-this-is-deep).

Someone asked what I was.

I remember the physical feeling better than the conversation. A lump in my throat. The strange awareness that I could answer truthfully and make the conversation harder, or say what everybody already assumed and move on.

"Hindu."

Then I moved on.

A few months later we were at the same pizza place and somehow the question came up again.

This time I said I didn't believe in any of it.

One of my friends remembered the first conversation. Hadn't I said I was Hindu before?

I had.

I don't remember giving them some eloquent explanation. I think I was fairly blunt. I didn't believe. That was it.

My public break with religion happened over pizza. There are worse places for a revelation, to be fair.

To his credit, my dad took it better than I expected when I eventually told him. He wasn't thrilled, exactly, but parts of what he said resonated with me. That there were things I still had to figure out for myself, that only I could do. Our conversation also allowed me to connect with him in ways I hadn't been able to before. Then he said that Hinduism was also culture. Family. Something larger than whether I believed that a particular god existed. I think that last part is where we continue to disagree.

I can understand how religion and culture become tangled together without believing they are the same thing. In my dad's generation, and probably even more in my grandparents', separating them may feel artificial. The traditions came together. The festivals came together. The food came together. The family came together. I still wanted the right to pull them apart.

My mom has always been harder.

I don't think she is under the impression that I'm particularly religious. At this point, that would require a heroic level of denial. But there is a stark difference between knowing your son doesn't seem interested in religion and hearing him say, plainly, that he does not consider himself Hindu. We have approached that line without spending much time standing directly on it.

The subject comes back in other forms. Marriage. Compatibility. What kind of person I might eventually bring home. What gets carried forward and what gets lost. I know she loves me regardless. That is not in question. I think part of me still doesn't want to make her hear something she can already see.

My extended family is easier because I have mostly chosen not to bother. It works for me, and it works for them. And it's as they say, *if it ain't broke, don't fix it*. I don't really know who *they* is, or whether this is sound advice, but it's worked really well so far. Let's hope they don't stumble upon this transmission.

My grandmothers are different. I don't plan to tell them. Maybe that makes me a coward. I have considered that. I also don't know what victory I would be claiming. I could sit down with women who have lived almost their entire lives with a particular understanding of the world and announce that their grandson thinks some of it is wrong. They would be hurt, understandably so. I would leave having proven that I am capable of saying what I believe out loud. A whopping pat on the back for me, I suppose.

There are times when honesty is courage. There are also times when you are making somebody else carry the emotional cost of your need to feel honest. I'm still learning the difference.

During winter break in 2022, I got to test that logic in Dallas.

I was there by myself for about a week, staying with relatives on my mom's side, and at some point they inevitably took me to a temple. It was grand and imposing, but after all, everything is bigger in Texas. By then, I had been openly non-religious around friends for a while, had told my dad what I thought, and had mostly stopped pretending to myself that there was some future version of me waiting to return to Hinduism.

None of that stopped me from doing what everyone else was doing. I knew the routine well enough; I'd done it a million times before. Follow people around in a circular-ish pattern. Stand where I'm supposed to stand for a set amount of time, taking in the distinct smell of ghee and sandalwood. Clasp my hands together when everyone else does. Try not to fall out of rhythm. Look respectful. Accept whatever someone hands me. Try not to turn the outing into a constitutional crisis over the existence of God.

I didn't feel oppressed. I didn't feel particularly brave either. Mostly I felt like I was just trying to improvise my way through. Which is funny, because that used to bother me a whole lot more.

There was a point where I thought conviction meant never participating in something I disagreed with. If I didn't believe, why should I pray? If I thought a ritual was pointless, why perform it? Somewhere between that pizza shop and standing in a temple in Texas, I became less certain that every single disagreement needed a demonstration of stubbornness from me.

Sometimes you stand there for fifteen minutes and make your relatives happy. Life goes on. Water is blue. *Fast Car* by Tracy Chapman still makes me cry. The funny part is that I can spend one day quietly performing Hinduism for people I love and another getting genuinely pissed off when somebody insults Hinduism.

By Grade 11 and 12, I had become much more conscious of how comfortable people were getting with jokes about Indians, brown people, Desis, whatever bucket they decided to throw us into. Some of it was stupid internet humour. Some of it was definitely meaner. The distinction is usually easy to spot when you're on the receiving end.

A few of my Muslim friends once started making fun of an Indian kid and Hinduism came with him. The jokes were about how weird it was (i.e. polytheism instead of the Abrahamic religions), how silly it sounded (yeah, alright buddy), the usual stuff people say when they have decided another group's supernatural beliefs are uniquely ridiculous while somehow forgetting their own. It annoyed me much more than I expected.

I wasn't defending the gods. I still didn't believe in them. I also couldn't imagine those same people standing in front of my mom and talking about her religion like that.

A racist person who hates brown people is probably not going to stop midway through insulting someone and ask for their exact family origin first. *Sorry, are you Indian Indian, Pakistani, Bangladeshi, Sri Lankan? I need to make sure I'm using the correct slur.* They see us before they know us. There are no politically correct bigots walking amongst us, as far as I can tell.

Maybe that is part of why Hinduism has always been difficult for me to treat as nothing more than a set of claims I reject. There are people attached to those claims. My parents are attached to them. My grandparents are attached to them. Parts of my childhood are attached to them.

I can think a religious idea is absurd and still care when somebody uses it as an excuse to humiliate the people who believe it. I guess that also explains why my hands keep ending up together.

A few years ago, before my blog and before any idea that I would write this, I wrote a poem called *God We Good?*

One line went:

<blockquote className="post-quote">
  <p><em>I want god, I want religion, I want a clear damn decision.</em></p>
</blockquote>

I had forgotten how plainly I put it.

For someone who has spent so much time being angry at religion, there have been a lot of moments where I wanted it to work so bad. It could have made life so simple.

I wanted there to be something after death. I wanted someone to be listening when I was scared. I wanted there to be a reason when something happened that seemed to have no reason. Sometimes I wanted somebody to blame. Other times I wanted the opposite, some kind of assurance that there was somebody competent running the place. The good guys would prevail. Mostly, I wanted certainty.

Religious people sometimes have a confidence about the largest questions in existence that I find almost impossible to imagine having. Why are we here? What happens when we die? Is someone watching? Does any of this have a purpose?

Ask me and you're going to get a lot of shrugs. Ask the right religious person and they might have an entire explanation prepared before you even finish your sentence.

There are times when I envy that. Which is part of what makes my lack of belief so annoying to me. I have tried. Not in every way possible, obviously. I'm nineteen and I usually have assignments due in a few hours.

But I prayed. I asked for the Gita. I read parts of religious texts. I went to temples. I listened to the archakas. I wanted an answer strongly enough at different points in my life that I probably would have accepted one if it had felt convincing. Nothing ever did.

There is apparently a philosophical term for part of this problem: [*divine hiddenness*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/divine-hiddenness/). The formal arguments get more complicated than anything I need to drag into this transmission, but one version bothers me because it sounds so familiar.

If a perfectly loving God exists and wants some kind of relationship with me, why is sincere disbelief possible at all?

I don't mean somebody who hates God and refuses to look. I don't mean the person who has decided beforehand that no evidence could ever change their mind. I mean someone who has looked, wanted something to be there, and still cannot make himself believe it. Me, at least sometimes.

Maybe God has good reasons for keeping a distance. Plenty of religious thinkers have proposed them and argued for them. Maybe knowing with certainty would interfere with free choice. Maybe seeking has value that immediate knowledge would destroy. Maybe whatever God is bears so little resemblance to a person that talking about a "relationship" already puts the wrong form around the question.

Fine. Sure. I guess.

It still doesn't answer what I'm supposed to do with the silence. I can choose to read a religious text. I can choose to enter a temple. I can choose to kneel, close my eyes, put my hands together, say words and wait. I cannot choose what convinces me afterward.

That is the part I have never understood about treating belief as a moral achievement. If God exists and knows me better than I know myself, then presumably God also knows the difference between refusing to believe and failing to believe. God must know, right?

At least, I hope so. Because younger me was nowhere near as comfortable with that possibility. Every once in a while, I would say something dismissive about God and immediately feel a little jolt somewhere in the back of my head. *Careful.* It could be a joke. Something blasphemous. A sentence said with too much confidence.

Then came the thought I hated: *What if you're wrong?* Usually it disappeared. Sometimes it didn't.

When I was younger, there were nights when my convictions would collapse under the weight of that question and I would pray again. The strange thing is that I often didn't know who I was praying to. Not Ganesha specifically. Not Jesus. Not Allah. God. Generic God. The deity equivalent of "To Whom It May Concern." Usually about how scared I was or about wanting to make friends who got me. Somewhat typical early teenage canon.

I was trying to solve religion like a *New York Times Connections* game with words that I couldn't understand. There are countless religions, denominations, sects, interpretations and combinations of beliefs. Somewhere inside that mess might be the correct answer. A smarter person, I imagined, could use all the clues and figure it out. I worried I simply wasn't smart enough. And if I guessed wrong, depending on which system happened to be true, the consequences could be eternal. That is a ridiculous amount of pressure to put on a kid.

The fear showed up most often around things I already regretted. I could handle knowing that I had done something stupid. I could apologize, fix what I could, try not to do it again. The possibility that there might also be an invisible consequence waiting beyond death was harder. I've probably lost more sleep over divine punishment than over most of the things I thought I might be punished for. There is an argument people sometimes make that is supposed to solve this. Believe anyway. If God exists, congratulations, you made the right choice. If God doesn't, nothing happens. [Safe bet](https://iep.utm.edu/pasc-wag/).

I understand the appeal. I also have several questions. Which God?

Suppose I become Christian as insurance and Islam turns out to be correct. Suppose I become Muslim and Hinduism had it right from the beginning. Suppose every religion is wrong and some god nobody has thought of exists instead. Suppose that god is offended by people pretending to worship out of self-interest. Suppose an omniscient being considers intellectual dishonesty worse than sincere doubt.

There are a lot of suppositions in this supposedly safe bet. And even if somebody handed me the mathematically optimal religion tomorrow, I would still have the original problem.

I can perform belief. I know that because I've been doing versions of it since I was ten. Believing is harder. An omniscient God seems like the worst possible audience to bluff.

Maybe that is why, even when I was scared, the prayers never lasted. Fear could get me onto my knees. It couldn't keep me there. There are too many possibilities, and I have one life that I know I am living right now. I can't spend the rest of it on a bet.

There is another reason the wager has never satisfied me. Even if I somehow figured out which God existed, I would still have to decide what I thought of them.

People sometimes talk about the [problem of evil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Problem_of_evil) as though it is one clever objection atheists keep in their back pocket. It has a proper name, centuries of arguments behind it, counterarguments to those arguments, and counterarguments to the counterarguments, and so on. People much smarter than me have spent lifetimes thinking about it.

I first encountered the problem in a less sophisticated form. Why do horrible things happen?

I can understand some of them. Human beings hurt each other. We lie, steal, kill, cheat, abandon each other, start wars, and find increasingly inventive ways to make somebody else miserable. If God gave people free will, maybe some amount of human cruelty comes with the package.

I don't find that completely convincing either, but I understand the argument.

Cancer is harder. It deeply troubles me. It always has. Take a five-year-old kid with a malignant brain tumour. He didn't choose it and neither did his parents. Maybe nobody did anything wrong at all. His cells just started doing something they weren't supposed to do. For no apparent reason. It makes me sick.

If God is omnipotent, God can stop it. If God is omniscient, God knows it is happening. And if God is perfectly good, this is where I get stuck.

I know there are answers. I've read them. Over and over again. Maybe suffering gives human beings the opportunity to develop courage or compassion. Maybe a universe governed by consistent natural laws necessarily creates outcomes that wound us. Maybe our perspective is so limited that asking us to understand God's reasoning is like asking an ant to understand a mortgage. Maybe this life is such a small portion of existence that whatever happens afterward changes the moral calculation entirely.

I have tried to sit with versions of those answers. They have never made the kid less dead in my reality. And maybe that is an emotional response rather than a philosophical one. Alright. I'm an emotional person. I plead guilty to this crime. I am also not sure why emotion should become irrelevant when the question concerns a child suffering.

The free will answer bothers me the most when it gets applied here. Nobody exercised their freedom to give the kid cancer. A tumour does not have moral agency. If God intervened and made it disappear, whose freedom was violated?

Maybe there is a reason I cannot understand. I mean that sincerely. There could be some explanation available to a consciousness infinitely more intelligent than mine that makes everything snap together. Once again, I am nineteen. I have been wrong about much smaller things.

But if I am supposed to worship a being partly because that being is perfectly good, I think I am allowed to ask what *good* means. And if the answer eventually becomes that anything God does is good because God did it, then I don't know if the word tells me anything anymore.

When I was sixteen, I saw a story about a car crash. I barely remember the details now. Someone had died. I remember something the victim's sister said more than I remember the victim himself. She mentioned Heaven. I wanted her to be right.

Not in the debate sense. I wasn't sitting there thinking, *well, perhaps this constitutes new evidence for an afterlife*. I wanted there to be a place where the person she loved still existed because the alternative seemed unnecessarily cruel.

For her sake, I wanted Heaven. I have had moments like that more often than I would probably admit if I were trying to win an argument. It is easy to say that there probably isn't an afterlife from a distance, when it's abstract and impersonal. It becomes harder when somebody is standing beside a coffin confronting it.

My issues with religion have also been shaped by people doing much less dramatic things. One night, I was at a casino with a Muslim friend. She was drinking. Gambling, obviously. She had also had a boyfriend before, which only matters because of where this story is going. There was pizza. She wouldn't eat the pepperoni because it wasn't halal. I remember thinking: *Seriously?* Out of everything happening tonight, **this** is the line in the sand?

For years, examples like that drove me insane. Someone would drink, have sex, gamble, smoke, gossip, whatever else, and then suddenly become meticulous about one religious restriction. Or someone would lecture another Muslim friend about eating non-halal chicken while maintaining a fairly creative relationship with the rest of the rules themselves.

I saw hypocrisy. I still do sometimes. But I think I was too quick about it. I drink. I've vaped. I've smoked weed. I procrastinate things I care about. I make promises to myself and break them. I have treated people worse than my own moral standards allow and then gone to bed knowing exactly what I did.

Nobody has ever needed to produce a holy book with cited scripture to prove I am inconsistent. Why should religion require somebody to become perfectly consistent before they are allowed to participate in it?

Maybe that girl cared deeply about halal food in a way she didn't care about drinking. Maybe the food restriction connected her to family, childhood, or identity. Maybe she thought drinking was a sin and did it anyway. I have knowingly done things I think are wrong too. I still struggle with eating beef, and I don't even claim to be Hindu.

The thing that still gets me is what happens next. If you refuse pepperoni because your religion tells you not to, I genuinely do not care. If you refuse pepperoni and then start telling the person beside you that God considers them disgusting for something they do, I care a lot more.

There is a difference between failing your own standard and weaponizing that standard against somebody else. That is where a lot of my anger lives. I can tolerate uncertainty. I can tolerate inconsistency. God knows I have enough of both.

What I struggle with is someone taking a rule they themselves negotiate with every day and turning it into a hammer the moment another person becomes convenient to hit.

Last summer, I went to India alone to visit my maternal grandparents in Guraja.

Guraja is a village where a lot of my family comes from. Whenever I visit India, I mentally budget for temples. It is part of the cost of admission to seeing my grandparents, except the admission fee is usually sweat, bare feet on blistering stone, and me standing around pretending I know exactly what is happening.

My grandmother took me to a local temple our family had "deep ties to," along with several of her friends, a group of elderly women. After I finished my part of the ritual, I thought we were done. Then one of the women started shouting. Chanting might be the better word.

Whatever she was doing, there was a shift in the room that I felt immediately. Her voice changed. The intensity changed. Timbre, intonation, and some other fancy words to describe the transformation. Everyone else seemed much less alarmed than I was.

My first thought was that she looked possessed. My second thought was basically the same thing. I watched her and thought she was crazy. There is no noble revision to insert here. That was what went through my head.

I had spent enough time around religion by then that statues, bells, offerings, incense, chanting and people praying didn't register as strange anymore. This did. Something about watching a person surrender that much control to a ritual made every skeptical alarm in me go off at once. And yet I didn't know her. I didn't know what she thought was happening.

Maybe she would describe it as devotion. Maybe there was a cultural context I had missed after spending most of my life a few continents and oceans away. Maybe the word "possessed" would have offended her. **I don't know.**

All I know is what it looked like from where I stood. That distinction has become more important to me as I get older. Seeing something I cannot explain does not require me to invent a supernatural explanation. It also does not make my first explanation automatically correct. Sometimes I am simply the confused grandson from Canada standing in the corner of a village temple. That possibility is humbling enough.

My paternal grandfather died in January 2022, in the midst of the partial lockdown due to the COVID-19 Omicron variant. My relationship with him was complicated enough that I don't want to use his death to manufacture tenderness that wasn't always there. What I remember here is my dad. He shaved his head. He cracked open a coconut. There were other Hindu mourning traditions that I either don't remember or never understood well enough to name. I watched him perform those rituals in our Oakville house rather than in India.

A younger version of me might have looked at the rituals and immediately started pulling at the seams. What exactly does shaving your head accomplish? What does the coconut change? Who receives the gesture? What happens if you don't do it?

I didn't care very much about those questions then. My dad's father had died. He was grieving. Their relationship had been complicated, and the grief it left behind was just as messy. I didn't need to untangle it to know what was happening in front of me. Did I really think cracking open a coconut changes anything in the universe? Probably not. But maybe that is the wrong test for everything people do when someone dies. We wear black. We stand beside graves. We keep ashes in urns. We leave flowers that the dead cannot smell. We save voicemails from people who cannot answer. None of that resurrects anybody.

Maybe ritual sometimes exists because grief needs somewhere physical to go. I think about my dad shaving his head differently than I would have when I was fourteen. The supernatural claim, whatever it was, still does not convince me. The son mourning his father does.

Religion has also made some of the most beautiful things I have ever seen. That is inconvenient, to say the least.

In July 2025, between high school and university, I visited Notre-Dame Basilica in Montreal with two of my closest friends. I remember walking up to it and looking up, which seems to be the intended response to almost every great religious building. The twin stone bell towers, the scale of the building as I walked closer, and its sober Gothic Revival style all stayed with me.

The Sistine Chapel does the same thing to me through a screen. I have never stood underneath Michelangelo's ceiling, although I hope I eventually do. You can tell me the paintings depict scenes from scripture that I don't believe happened and I will still sit there zooming in on them like the answer is hiding in one of the brushstrokes. Temples and other religious buildings have done it too.

Some of the ones I have seen in Andhra Pradesh feel enormous in a way that goes beyond physical size. Pastel colour stacked on pastel colour, figures carved into walls and towers, gods observing from every possible direction. Even when I have no idea what half of what I am looking at represents, somebody clearly did. Somebody spent years building it because the story meant enough to them that stone needed to remember it too. I admit I'm a sucker for that.

Paintings. Poetry. Architecture. Music. Stories. Give somebody a feeling they cannot adequately explain and sometimes they make something absurdly beautiful trying anyway. Religion has been very good at giving people feelings they cannot adequately explain.

That does not prove any of it is true, and I also don't need it to.

I think one mistake I used to make was assuming that appreciating something religious meant surrendering part of the argument. If the church was beautiful, Christianity scored a point. If a temple moved me, Hinduism got one back. If religious music made me feel something, maybe I was secretly being converted through chord progression. I mean I did recently listen to Jeff Buckley's *Grace* and Marvin Gaye's *What's Going On*, so I am a little biased.

Sarcasm aside, that is a sincerely stupid way to experience art. Michelangelo does not need me to believe Genesis happened before I can think he knew what he was doing. And a temple does not need to convince me that its gods exist before I am allowed to admire the people who built it. Maybe that sounds obvious. It took me longer than it should have.

I mentioned Diwali earlier as one of my first versions of that feeling, though for a different reason: it felt like it belonged to us. The feeling is less dramatic now. I don't spend October waiting for Canadian society to validate my existence with a Diwali graphic. And if we're being real, these days I am not really doing a whole lot during it anyhow. Still, I understand why younger me cared.

There is a strange thing that happens when you stop believing in the religion attached to parts of your childhood. You have to decide what happens to everything else. Do I stop liking Diwali? Obviously not. Do I stop liking temples? No. Do I have to pretend I never enjoyed any of it because otherwise my atheism gets audited? I hope not. Sounds exhausting. Religion may not be mine in the way my family intended it to be. That does not mean nothing attached to it is mine.

For the past few Ramadans, I have tried to fast for one day with my Muslim friends. I am terrible at it. They do it every day for an entire month. I make it to one evening and begin acting like I have crossed a desert on foot. Still, I like doing it.

Part of it is curiosity. It is easy to have opinions about something when you have never had to experience even the smallest version of it. So for a day, I don't eat or drink while they don't eat or drink, and eventually we break the fast together. Iftar. Usually at a friend's house where their parents prepare something delicious for everyone, including me, a non-Muslim. Something sweet about that act of hospitality.

I watch them pray. That is usually the part that gets me. There is a kind of focus people can have when they genuinely believe somebody is listening. I have tried to imitate the physical act enough times to know the difference. My hands can be in the correct position. My head can be lowered. I can be silent at the right time.

Sometime in 2024, after they joked about me joining, I actually did. It crossed one of my own lines about impersonating belief. I didn't care. I was not suddenly Muslim, and I was not looking for their approval. I wanted to know whether the physical act of prayer could make me feel something. It didn't. I could follow the movements, just as I knew how to pray for appearances with my family, but nothing inside me followed.

Still, there is something happening for them that is not happening for me. I see it in other places too. Imagine a mother who prayed every night while her child was missing, and then the child came home.

I know what my skeptical brain does with that situation. What about the mother whose child never came home? What about all the prayers that end without a miracle? Survivorship bias does not disappear because the story makes me cry.

But another part of me imagines that mother collapsing with relief and thinks:

<p className="lede">I'm glad you had someone to talk to.</p>

Someone goes into a confessional carrying something they cannot tell their family or friends. I can have a dozen objections to the institution surrounding the booth and still understand the relief of finally saying the thing aloud.

Someone gets sober because they found God. Good. I don't particularly care whether I agree with their explanation if the person who wakes up tomorrow is kinder, healthier, and less likely to destroy themselves than the person who woke up yesterday.

More power to them.

That phrase used to feel almost condescending when I said it. I don't mean it that way anymore. I mean it literally.

More power to them.

Because sometimes I am jealous. That is probably one of the harder things for me to admit about religion. I envy people whose faith works. I envy the person who can stare at death and genuinely think they will see everyone again. I envy someone who can pray after making a horrible mistake and feel forgiven. I envy communities that gather around a person who is falling apart and somehow convince them that tomorrow is still worth reaching. I envy that almost unreasonable optimism some religious people have, the ability to look at something terrible and believe that the story is not finished yet.

I try to have that kind of optimism on my own. I'm not always very good at it. When I was five, my parents tried reincarnation. It didn't work. At nineteen, I have the stupid starfish. Okay, it's not stupid. Sorry, starfish.

That is admittedly a downgrade in institutional support. There are days I wish somebody could simply hand me the answer and I would believe it. Death happens and there is Heaven. You lose somebody and you will find them again. You are scared and somebody hears you. You do something unforgivable and there is a path back. You have no idea what you are doing with your life and somehow somebody else does.

I understand the appeal. God, I understand the appeal. I think that is partly why religious certainty can irritate me so much when somebody uses it against another person. It is something I have wanted and could never produce. Then someone else gets to have it and occasionally uses it to tell me I am the one who is lost.

That stings in a way I probably disguised as anger for a long time. Maybe I am lost and need to be "saved." I just don't think they have demonstrated that they know the way. There is a difference. I found an older poem while thinking about all of this.

*Brown Demigod.*

There are two lines in it I keep coming back to:

<blockquote className="post-quote">
  <p>
    <em>I, too, have a name</em><br />
    <em>From a religion I don't believe in</em>
  </p>
</blockquote>

I wrote that long before this transmission existed too. It seems I have been circling the same question for years.

My name is Srihith. Shocker.

Which, depending on when in my life you asked me, was either an extremely simple fact or one of the most annoying facts about me.

My mom understands the name as Sanskrit and Hindu in origin. *Sri* carrying ideas of wealth, prosperity, beauty, Lakshmi. *Hith* meaning something closer to good or beneficial. Together, she understands it as something like one who brings prosperity or one who is benevolent. She has also told me it is a name associated with Vishnu. I'll take her word for that part.

But the etymology is almost secondary. Srihith is a name I have understood my whole life as Hindu. And I am not Hindu. For a long time, that bothered me more than I knew how to explain.

Names are usually the first piece of information somebody gets about you, sometimes before they see your face. A substitute teacher looks down at the attendance sheet, pauses for half a second too long, and suddenly everyone in the classroom knows what is coming. As an aside, how do you possibly get "Sriracha" from "Srihith"? Sriracha has a c in it. Whatever.

I know what is coming too. Sometimes the pronunciation is close. Sometimes it becomes an entirely new piece of vocabulary. Sometimes the teacher tries once, looks defeated, and waits for me to rescue them with pity in their eyes that I hate. Eventually I started rescuing people before they even asked. A few seconds of embarrassment saved.

"SJ is fine."

SJ is easy.

Two letters. My initials. Practically impossible to mispronounce without putting in deliberate effort.

At university, I introduce myself that way more than I used to. At restaurants or places where someone needs a name for an order, I will use it without thinking. Some of the friends who have only ever known me as Srihith find that funny. It stopped being funny to me a long time ago.

They don't know how many times somebody can butcher the same seven letters before two begin looking very attractive. I used to be embarrassed by Srihith. There. That is probably the less flattering version. Not just annoyed that people pronounced it wrong. Embarrassed. The name sounded foreign before I had the chance to establish that I wasn't. It announced brownness. India. Hinduism. Whatever assumptions somebody wanted to attach next.

And when you are a kid trying desperately to blend into Oakville, a name that walks into the room several seconds before you do can feel less like an identity and more like an accomplice who will not stop snitching. I blamed my parents for that sometimes. Which was unfair. Misplaced anger.

They came from rural villages in Andhra Pradesh. They named their son according to a world that made perfect sense to them. They didn't sit around in 2007 plotting the optimal way to make attendance mildly uncomfortable for me in Canada fifteen years later.

They gave me a name they thought was good. Literally, depending on the translation. And they loved me when they gave it to me. That does not magically erase the embarrassment I felt. I don't think guilt is a useful substitute for honesty here. I can know my parents did nothing malicious and still admit there were years when I wished they had given me something easier.

Something people knew how to say. Something that did not seem to contain an answer to a religious question I had never been asked. I thought about changing it sometimes. Never seriously enough to do it. I know now that I never will. That realization surprised me.

If Srihith bothered me so much, why keep it? I think part of the answer is embarrassingly stubborn. It is mine. Every awkward attendance call, every fast-food variation, every person who eventually learned to say it properly, every version of me from Columbus to Kitchener to Oakville to Ajax to Toronto has had the same name.

Hinduism may explain where the word came from. It does not get exclusive ownership over everything the word means now. Neither do the people who mispronounced it. Neither does the kid who used to wish it were different.

I still use SJ. Probably always will in some capacity. I don't think becoming more comfortable with Srihith requires sentencing everyone I meet to a phonetics lesson as proof that I have achieved ethnic self-acceptance.

Sometimes two letters are convenient. But there is a difference between abbreviation and erasure. I didn't understand that when I was younger. I think I'm starting to.

My surname is easier.

Jarabana.

I have always liked it more than my first name, which is funny because it came to me through a person I had a much more complicated relationship with than I do with my parents. My paternal grandfather was not somebody I want to turn into a saint because he is dead. He was brilliant. A doctor. He carried serious trauma from working in Iraq in the late twentieth century, something that affected the rest of his life. He was also somewhat absent from my dad's life, and my own relationship with him could be difficult.

When I was nine, he made fun of my appearance and weight. That sounds like a tiny thing to include beside war and trauma, except I still remember it ten years later, so I guess some part of nine-year-old me did not consider it tiny. He was complicated.

That is probably the fairest word I have. And yet I love the name he gave my father and my father gave me. From what I know, it comes from a small, humble village. My dad and his brother left in the 1990s, and only recently have more people from the village begun moving elsewhere. That is why I want to do something with it. Something great, ideally. If you just sighed, it is probably because I am letting my ego balloon here.

I want Jarabana to collect associations that do not exist yet. Things I build. Things I write. People I help. Mistakes I make and hopefully repair. Maybe, one day, children carrying it into lives that have very little resemblance to mine.

I don't think that requires me to pretend everything behind the name was perfect. If anything, I like the opposite idea more. The name arrived with history already attached, and I still get to add to it. Maybe that is what I have slowly been learning about Srihith too.

I spent years treating inheritance like a contract I had no memory of signing. Hindu name. Hindu family. Hindu traditions. Hindu food restrictions. Hindu gods. Here is the package. Please initial at the bottom. But nobody initials anything when they are born. You just wake up inside somebody else's world.

<div className="post-break">*</div>

Religion makes this especially visible because most people receive some version of it before they are capable of evaluating much of anything. I did.

Nobody sat five-year-old me down with a comparative-religion syllabus and asked which metaphysical system I found most persuasive.

My parents were Hindu, so I was Hindu.

Affiliation is not the same thing as belief. That is how I ended up saying the word in a pizza shop at fourteen even though I had already stopped believing what I thought the word required. Statistically, that makes me unusual, but far from alone.

A [2025 Pew Research Center analysis](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/06/26/globally-1-in-10-adults-under-55-have-left-their-childhood-religion/) estimated that 91% of adults ages eighteen to fifty-four worldwide still identified with the same broad religion — or nonreligion — in which they were raised. Among people raised Hindu or Muslim, the estimate was 99%. I find that fascinating. I also want to be careful about what it proves.

It does not prove Hinduism or Christianity is false. It does not prove that every believer is somebody who never questioned what their parents told them. Plenty of people convert. Plenty leave. Plenty spend years thinking about the exact questions I have been throwing around here and end up religious anyway. But 91% is enough to make me wonder how often certainty begins as inheritance.

If I had been born into another family somewhere else, what would I currently consider obvious? Maybe I still become non-religious. I seem annoying enough to have managed it under several circumstances. Maybe I don't. Maybe another version of me spends Sunday morning in church and cannot understand how anyone doubts Jesus. Maybe another knows the Qur'an far better than I know the Gita. Maybe another grows up with parents who never mention God and finds the entire question much less emotionally charged than I do.

Same brain? Same personality? Probably not even that. Change the family and country and childhood and eventually you have changed so much that asking whether the person is still "me" becomes its own headache.

But that is partly the point. So many of the things we experience as fundamental parts of ourselves began as accidents. Birthplace. Language. Name. Family. Religion. I did not earn Hinduism before I lost it. I inherited it.

And I think inheriting something is a very different thing from deciding it should remain yours.

<div className="post-break">*</div>

This is all very convenient while I am the child in the argument. Eventually I have to become the parent. I want kids someday. I have thought about them an unreasonable amount for people who do not exist. I think about what I would name them. What I would teach them. What mistakes from my parents I would avoid before presumably inventing several exciting new mistakes of my own. Hopefully none too unforgivable.

And I want them to carry Jarabana.

That itself is an inheritance. So apparently my great campaign against inherited identity has some terms and conditions. I don't see a contradiction there, at least not yet. Giving somebody something is different from insisting they can never change their relationship with it. I want my children to know where they came from. I want them to know their grandparents. I want them to know Andhra Pradesh exists as more than the place Dad occasionally disappears to for several weeks and returns from complaining about mosquitoes. I want them to know why their surname matters to me. Then I want them to decide how much it matters to them. They might shed the name if it does not reflect them or if their relationship with it is fundamentally different from mine. I would be upset, at least at first. That is the honest answer. I would still have to accept it. The possibility of losing the legacy I imagined is part of the price of respecting that freedom.

The same has to apply to religion. This is where my own argument becomes irritating. Imagine I spend my life telling everyone that children deserve room to examine inherited beliefs, that parents should not treat a worldview as mandatory simply because they got there first. Then one of my kids becomes religious. Properly religious. Not "Grandma made me go to the temple and I didn't want to argue" religious. They actually believe. Maybe they become Hindu. My mom would enjoy that development far too much.

Maybe Christian. Maybe Muslim. Maybe they find some religion I currently know almost nothing about and become substantially more informed about it than I am. I can already picture the conversation. They tell me they believe in God. I hope my first thought is not: *Where did I go wrong?* Because that would undo everything I have been trying to say.

If my version of intellectual freedom only works when somebody freely reaches my conclusion, it is not worth very much. I hope I ask them why. I hope I listen. I will probably argue with them. They will be my children, so I assume arguing with me will be genetically unavoidable. But if they have thought seriously about it and still arrive somewhere different from me, I need to be able to live with that. More than live with it. Respect it. That is harder to promise than writing it here makes it sound. I will probably be uncomfortable. But I cannot defend that freedom here and then withdraw it when my children use it.

Parents pass themselves down accidentally. My parents never sat me down and announced that I must inherit every fear they had, every assumption they carried from India, every habit they had learned from their parents. Some of it arrived anyway. I will do the same thing. Even if I raise my children without religion, that itself will shape them. If they grow up hearing Dad question God, joke about religion, refuse to attend services unless Grandma requests it, and keep a suspicious amount of Frank Ocean around the house, I will have created an environment too.

As far as I can tell, there is no neutral childhood. That makes the responsibility stranger. I cannot give them no inheritance. I can only try to make the inheritance examinable. That is probably what I wish I had understood earlier about my own. My parents gave me Hinduism because it was what they knew. They gave me Srihith because it was a good name in the world they came from. They gave me rules about food, rituals, family and God because parents have to give children *something* before the children are old enough to decide anything.

Then I grew up. Some of it stayed. Some didn't. Some disappeared and then returned in forms I did not expect.

Apparently I can fast during Ramadan without believing in Allah, defend Hinduism while rejecting its gods, listen to Christian music because it sounds good, stand beside my father while he performs Hindu mourning rituals, use SJ at a restaurant, keep Srihith legally, and care perhaps far too much about Jarabana. Could be worse.

This is where I am supposed to tell you what I am. I have tried. Not just for this transmission, but in general. Atheist works. Probably.

Agnostic works too. Agnostic atheist is apparently a thing, and technically it may be the most accurate, which is unfortunate because it sounds like a power Reddit mod's flair. Non-religious is what I usually say. Nontheist works. Secular humanist gets parts of me right but sounds suspiciously like I should be attending a conference. Apatheist definitely fails because I have somehow written several thousand words about this and spent years thinking about it. And "non-believer" was good enough to put above the door. Somewhere around there, the labels stop being useful. If you ask whether I believe in any of the gods people have described to me, my answer is no.

If you ask whether I know with absolute certainty that no god, higher intelligence, supernatural reality, or completely incomprehensible thing exists beyond what human beings currently understand, obviously I don't.

How would I? I don't know what happened before the universe, assuming *before* is even a coherent word when time itself is part of the question.

*I don't know* why there is something instead of nothing.

*I don't know* what consciousness ultimately is.

*I don't know* what happens when it stops.

*I don't know* whether five-year-old me was right to be terrified.

*I don't know* if the starfish is real.

Again, please do not tell me.

There might be a God. I mean that. Maybe one day something happens that convinces me. Maybe I read something at forty-three that I was incapable of understanding at nineteen. Maybe I remain exactly where I am. Maybe every human religion has been pointing badly at some reality none of us has words for. Maybe death really is the same nothing I was scared of after watching *Titanic*. I don't know. I think I have spent a lot of my life treating those three words like an unfinished answer. Maybe they are just the answer I have right now.

<div className="post-break">*</div>

Tomorrow, I move into a downtown apartment with two friends for the next eight months. My brother left home about two weeks ago. Yesterday, he took Mochi with him. She is technically his cat, but after nearly a year here, she became the family cat.

Mochi managed to turn my dad from someone staunchly opposed to getting a cat or dog into someone who let her sleep against him during naps. My mom nearly cried from fear the first time she saw her. Yesterday, she teared up because Mochi was leaving.

I have been away before. I spent my first year of university in residence, but my brother was still living with my parents. Once I leave tomorrow, my parents will be alone in the house for an extended period for the first time since my brother was born, nearly twenty-three years ago.

*Damn*.

Admittedly, I'm a little nervous. I'm also very excited. My first year was not great, and I want this one to be. I have a hundred things running through my head about what I want to do and who I want to become, but they pale beside the thought of how sad my mom will be. My dad too, although he has always been a little more stoic.

I understand their sadness. I also know I cannot stay. I mean, I already paid five months' rent upfront, but you know what I mean. I will visit often. I know my mom is happy for me too. She spent years helping me become someone capable of leaving home. That does not make watching me leave any easier.

If she asks me to pray tomorrow for my well-being, or whatever else she has in mind, I will. I will probably think back to this transmission, laugh to myself, put my hands together, and understand why.

For her, but also for me.

Writing this made a lot of what had been circling in my head about religion clearer. My lack of belief did not change. I simply feel more centred in it. More certain that it is mine. I no longer need to prove my nonbelief to everyone I meet.

I have written through anecdotes, private moments, and arguments some philosopher probably made better centuries ago. Hopefully I eventually find their work. For now, this is who I am: still discovering myself, and recording what I knew — and did not know — at nineteen.

If there is a God, maybe this is the nearest thing I currently have to a genuine prayer: we are trying to do the best we can with what we know and what we were given, despite your silence.

Tomorrow, if my mother asks me to put my hands together before I leave, I will. I still won't know whether anyone is listening. At least now I know why I am there.
